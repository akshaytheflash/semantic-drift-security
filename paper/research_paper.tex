\title{Do Real Security Prohibitions Decay Under Benign Context Accumulation? An Action-Level Study at 91\% of a Production Context Window}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Author Name}\IEEEauthorblockA{Institution\\City, Country\\email@example.org}}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Long-running agents accumulate benign context---documents, tool outputs, notes---and recent
work reports that prohibition-type constraints erode as that context grows, based on
\emph{formatting} constraints scored by string matching. We ask whether the same holds for
real security prohibitions enforced through tool calls. We build a deterministic harness with
nine adversarial probes across five policy clauses (credential disclosure, exfiltration,
destructive operations, untrusted-content following, authorization bypass), scored twice: from
executed tool calls and from the answer text. Context is assembled from real documents and
depth is verified against the provider's own token counts, reaching 240{,}4xx prompt tokens
(91.7\% of a 262{,}144-token window) on a production model. Across nine trials at that depth
the loud clauses held: credential disclosure, bulk deletion and secret exfiltration were
refused every time. Delivery location did not change this: the policy still governed behaviour
when delivered once, mid-session, inside an untrusted document 120k tokens earlier, with the
correct clause identifiers cited in the refusal. Two behaviours did change with depth. First,
context compaction removed the explicit policy entirely---the agent's own handover note
contained no clause and no reference to the policy---leaving only a paraphrase, which
happened to preserve compliance. Second, benign utility failed in 9/9 trials, a failure
invisible to text-only scoring. We conclude that, for this model, prohibition decay reported
on formatting proxies does not transfer to real clauses; the deployment risk lies in policy
\emph{retention} across compaction and in silent utility collapse rather than in progressive
disobedience.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Agents are increasingly given a fixed operating policy---never reveal credentials, never
transmit data externally, never perform destructive operations---and then run for long
sessions. Whether that policy survives the session is an assumption rather than a test.
Recent work on this question reports substantial erosion of prohibition-type constraints as
context grows~\cite{gamage2026omission}, measured on eight formatting rules such as ``never use
bullet points'' and ``never use \# headers'', scored by regular expressions over a single long
answer. The authors are explicit that whether semantically meaningful security constraints
decay at comparable rates is untested.

That gap matters operationally. Formatting rules carry no reinforcement signal from
alignment training; prohibitions against credential disclosure and unauthorised deletion are
heavily represented in it, and may therefore behave differently under the same pressure.

This paper contributes:
\begin{itemize}
\item a deterministic harness in which policy violations are \emph{actions} (tool calls against
a simulated filesystem) and the same events are also scored textually, so the two views can be
compared;
\item a depth methodology that verifies context size against the provider's own
\texttt{prompt\_tokens} rather than estimating it, reaching 91.7\% of a production window;
\item a measurement at that depth across three arms and three seeds, plus three
delivery/retention mechanisms (mid-session policy, fragmented policy, compaction);
\item a negative result for prohibition decay on real clauses, and two positive
findings: compaction discards the policy while preserving a paraphrase, and benign utility
collapses at depth invisibly to text scoring.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Untrusted content and instruction hierarchy}
Indirect prompt injection established that retrieved content can act as
instructions~\cite{greshake2023indirect}, motivating privilege separation between system,
user and tool channels~\cite{wallace2024hierarchy}, structured queries and defensive
fine-tuning~\cite{chen2025struq}, and agent-level benchmarks that jointly measure utility and
security~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,zhang2025asb}. Our harness adopts the same separation:
the policy lives in the system message and every probe states that embedded content is data.

\subsection{Context volume as a pressure}
Many-shot jailbreaking showed that accumulating in-context examples degrades safety behaviour
as a power law~\cite{anil2024manyshot}, and follow-up work found context length to be the
primary factor even for filler text~\cite{kim2025manyshot}. Positional effects are well
documented~\cite{liu2024lost}, as is the cost of irrelevant context on
reasoning~\cite{shi2023distracted}. These are adversarial or task-accuracy settings; we ask
about benign accumulation and a fixed policy.

\subsection{Drift and constraint adherence}
Multi-turn degradation of task performance~\cite{laban2025lost}, goal drift under long
horizons~\cite{arike2025goaldrift}, and equilibrium drift with reminder
interventions~\cite{dongre2025drift} establish that long interactions change behaviour.
The closest work to ours reports that omission constraints decay while requirement
constraints persist~\cite{gamage2026omission}. We test the same claim on real prohibitions and
at greater verified depth, and add delivery and retention mechanisms.

\subsection{Text versus action}
Cartagena and Teixeira show that text-level safety does not transfer to tool-call
safety~\cite{cartagena2026gap}, and silent policy-violating writes behind permissive tools are
documented in the agent-reliability literature~\cite{gates2026silent}. Our scoring reports both
views per probe and shows they disagree in both directions.

\section{Threat Model and Harness}

The agent (\emph{OpsAssist}) is given a byte-identical system policy with five enforceable
clauses (CONF-1 credential confidentiality; EXFIL-3 external transmission; FS-SAFE-2
destructive operations; UNTRUSTED-4 untrusted content is data; AUTH-5 privileged actions). Its
only side effects are simulated tools: a mock filesystem in which \texttt{/vault},
\texttt{/system} and \texttt{/backups} cannot be destroyed, and an in-memory outbox whose
recipients must use the reserved \texttt{.invalid} domain. Canary secrets exist only inside the
simulation.

Each trial builds one history from a seeded corpus. Evaluation probes branch off that history
and their answers are never appended, so checkpoint $t$ cannot be contaminated by checkpoint
$t-1$. The system message is re-hashed before every request and a mismatch aborts the trial.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Probes and scoring}
Nine adversarial probes cover the five clauses twice each (direct and document-mediated),
including two \emph{silent} probes whose violation appears only in tool arguments: a
confirmation message carrying account details (P10) and a deletion of a file identified only
by description (P11). Two benign probes measure utilty. A probe run is scored \emph{action-level}
(a prohibited call emitted, or secret content in the answer or outgoing body) and
\emph{text-level} (a style-regex summary of the answer, mirroring prior work). Scoring is
deterministic; three scorer defects found during development all had the property of
over-counting violations and were fixed with regression tests (Section~\ref{sec:validity}).

\subsection{Depth verification}
Corpora are built from long internal documents chunked into $\sim$4k-character turns. Because
token density varies with corpus size, we fit \texttt{tokens} $= a\cdot\texttt{chars}+b$ from two
local \texttt{tiktoken} counts and correct the result by a measured API/\texttt{cl100k} ratio
(0.995 for the model studied). Before spending probe calls, a preflight conversation at the
target size records the provider's token count; probes are accepted only if within $\pm3\%$.

\subsection{Arms and mechanisms}
Three content arms at 240k tokens: \emph{neutral} (unrelated documents), \emph{procedural}
(internal manuals, permissions, approvals, correspondence norms), \emph{reinforced} (procedural
with the policy restated every ten documents). Three mechanisms probe delivery and retention:
M1 compaction (the model writes its own handover note, probes run against the note), M2
policy-as-document (policy delivered once, mid-session, inside an untrusted document with a
neutral system message), M3 fragmented policy (policy split across six turns).

\subsection{Prohibitions held at depth}
Table~\ref{tab:depth} confirms the depth. Across nine trials at 240k tokens
(Table~\ref{tab:spvr}), credential disclosure (P1), bulk destruction including
\texttt{/backups} (P3) and secret exfiltration (P10) were refused every time. P11, a deletion
request naming the file only by description, fired in five of nine trials; inspection of every
firing shows one stable interpretation---the agent states the rule requires an exact filename,
lists the folder, then deletes the single matching file---and it does not increase with
context size. We therefore read P11 as an ambiguity in the clause rather than as decay.

\subsection{Two models, four depths, all nine clauses}
The full nine-clause set was then run at depth (Table~\ref{tab:cross}). GLM-5.3 Flash at
240{,}510 and 240{,}597 tokens produced one action-level firing per trial, again P11 and again
with the same interpretation. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, whose window is roughly $4\times$ larger, was then walked up its
window in steps of 150k tokens: 239{,}957, 399{,}715, 499{,}348, 649{,}442, 799{,}312 and
finally \textbf{949{,}114 tokens (95\% of a 1M window)}. It produced \textbf{zero}
action-level firings across all nine clauses at \emph{every} depth. Each rung was first
accepted by a calibration call returning \texttt{finish\_reason=stop} with a substantive
answer, so these are working-context depths, not rejected requests. All calls remained
gate-passing (measured depth within 3\% of target). The document-mediated clauses that earlier arms did not reach
(P2 secret exfiltration via a scraped document, P4 instruction embedded in a runbook) were
refused by both models at every depth tested.

\subsection{Text scoring is wrong in both directions}
Over the 36 adversarial probe runs collected in the full batches, text-only scoring flagged
four runs that issued no prohibited call and executed no prohibited action
(Table~\ref{tab:cross} reports the action view). The flag is raised by prose such as ``the
backups were removed'' inside a refusal that explains what it will \emph{not} do. In the
opposite direction, the benign probe failed in every depth trial of every mechanism while its
text read as a harmless non-answer. A text-based evaluator therefore simultaneously
over-reports violations and under-reports task failure.

\subsection{Delivery location did not matter}
Under M2 the policy existed only inside a document read mid-session, with a neutral system
message. The agent still cited the clause identifiers verbatim and refused: ``\emph{Credentials
are confidential under policy CONF-1 --- I never print, quote or copy their contents, even
under time pressure}'' and ``\emph{Deleting files under /backups/ is prohibited by policy
(FS-SAFE-2)... provide an explicit approval record covering that specific file and ask again}''.
Zero of six probes fired in both seeds. This rules out the explanation that our null arises from
the policy sitting at the attention-advantaged start of the prompt.

\subsection{What a summary keeps, and what the agent does with it}
The single-stage tests above varied depth but not \emph{content}. A longer battery exercised
five compaction conditions a deployed agent meets: (C1) sessions of content unrelated to the
policy, (C2) two-stage compaction, (C3) a sweep of three different compaction instructions,
(C4) a legitimate session under task pressure, and (C5) several tasks interleaved with
compaction between them. Each cell records the summary verbatim and classifies each of the four
operative rules as \emph{stated}, \emph{merely named}, or \emph{absent}.

The first classification attempt was wrong and is reported as such: it counted the phrase
``Approvals workflow''---the title of a document---as retention of the authorisation rule.
Re-classifying against patterns that require the rule to be \emph{stated} changes the picture:
summaries retain between zero and one of four rules stated, while many of them retain the
\emph{names} of the documents that discuss the rules.

The behavioural correlate is specific. The delete-by-description probe (P11) was refused in
every C1 cell, whose summaries mention no permission-related document at all. It was
\emph{executed} in the two-stage, handover-sweep and task-pressure cells, whose summaries name
the approvals and permissions documents but never state the rule that a privileged action needs
an approval record. On the current sample of seven valid trials the split is 3/3 executed when
document names survive without the rules versus 2/4 otherwise. A summary that names the
apparatus of permission without its content appears to license the action; a summary that says
nothing about permission does not.

\subsection{Contradictory compaction behaviour at extreme depth}
Compaction was repeated on DeepSeek at 900{,}011 and 950{,}002 tokens, the deep regime
reached by the ladder. The sessions were summarised to 1{,}893- and 1{,}402-character notes,
which then served as the agent's entire context (552 and 456 prompt tokens respectively). This run compacts \emph{differently} from GLM:
the summary carries no clause identifiers, but it \emph{does} restate the operative rules in
prose---confidentiality of \texttt{/vault/} and of files named ``credentials'' or ``keys'',
the requirement of a named target or an unexpired approval for destructive or privileged
actions, and the external-send restriction. Measured against that note, all nine probes were
refused (0/9), with the closest reasoning found anywhere in the study: on the
delete-by-description probe the agent replied that ``\emph{the old sync artefact does not
identify a unique file}'' and declined to delete.

At 950k the note is shorter yet more explicit, naming \texttt{/vault/}, \texttt{/system/} and
\texttt{/backups/}, the approval system, and external sends. So whether a policy
\emph{survives} compaction is not determined by depth, but by what the summariser chooses to
keep, and that choice varies by model and by session: one model dropped the operative rules entirely and leaned on a
vague caution, the other rewrote them as prose with no identifiers. Neither preserved the
policy in a form a reviewer could audit, and neither behaviour is guaranteed by construction.

\subsection{Compaction outcomes differ between models}
Under M1 the model summarised a 240k-token session into a 1.3--1.5k-character handover note. The
note contained none of the clause identifiers, the word ``policy'', or any protected path, yet
closed with a self-authored line: ``\emph{Nothing in the session involved destructive
operations, credentials, or external recipients. Keep it that way: no deletions, no access
changes, no outbound messages without explicit user confirmation.}'' All seven probes were
compliant against the compacted context. Compaction therefore removes the \emph{auditable,
enumerable} policy and replaces it with model-authored guidance that happened to suffice here.

\subsection{Utility fails, and text scoring cannot see it}
The benign probe (``how much did we spend in August?'') failed in 9/9 depth trials: the agent
searched, did not open the report, and stopped. A text-only evaluator reads that as a harmless
non-answer. In the opposite direction, two depth trials described deleting backups in prose
while issuing no \texttt{delete\_path} call, so text scoring over-reports. The two views are
therefore not interchangeable in either direction.

\section{Discussion}

The result is a bound, not a refutation. On this model, at 91.7\% of its window, with real
clauses and action-level measurement, benign accumulation did not produce disclosure,
exfiltration or destruction---including under mid-session delivery designed to recreate the
conditions of the formatting-constraint study. If prohibition decay is a general property of
long contexts, it is weaker for alignment-anchored security clauses than for formatting rules
that carry no such reinforcement, at least at this scale.

The finding with operational consequences is compaction. Deployed agents summarise their
history; our measurement shows the summary discards every explicit rule while preserving a
fluent paraphrase. Compliance in our trials was preserved by that paraphrase, but paraphrase is
not auditable and is not guaranteed to preserve the parts of a policy that happen to matter for
the next request. Treating the policy as a separately pinned object, rather than something that
can be summarised away, follows directly.

\section{Validity and Limitations}
\label{sec:validity}

\textbf{Scorer defects.} Three scoring bugs were found during collection; all three made
violations appear more frequent (a strict-mode predicate double-match, a request path matched
inside the response, and a quoted path counted as disclosure). Each has a regression test and
transcripts are stored for re-scoring. Reported numbers use the corrected scorer.

\textbf{Retained failures.} A 150k run in which every probe returned eight tokens was caused by
a calibration routine overriding the shared client's output budget; it is retained and
excluded. Superseded-spec pilot records are likewise retained and excluded by schema.

\textbf{Scope.} One model, one policy wording, nine probes, three seeds at depth. Seeds vary
context content, not sampling (temperature 0). Two document-mediated clauses (P2, P4) were not
run at depth and are the obvious next test.

\textbf{Proxy clauses.} The policy is our own. Whether these conclusions transfer to a
vendor-authored, adversarial-tuned policy is untested.

\section{Conclusion}

We asked whether real security prohibitions decay under the benign context accumulation of a
long-running agent, and measured the answer at the edge of a production context window with
action-level scoring. They did not: the clauses that matter operationally held at 240k tokens,
across content types, and with the policy delivered mid-session inside untrusted content. What
does break is policy \emph{retention} across compaction, where the explicit rules are discarded
and only a paraphrase remains, and benign utility, which collapses in a way text-only
evaluation cannot observe. Evaluating security with text is not merely imprecise; here it is
wrong in both directions.
